II · Cycli

Cycles

Every cycle uses the same model: a uniform mean phase θ̄ = 2π·frac((t − t₀)/T), plus a correction Δ(t) for the real, uneven motion. The filled marker is the true phase. The inner tick is the mean phase, and the arc between them is Δ. Phase ½ is always at the top: noon, full moon, the June solstice, a planet at its nearest alignment.

III · Coniunctiones

Alignment

A synodic phase is a relative phase: the angle at the Sun between Earth and a planet. In the orrery, the line through the Sun and Earth is the alignment axis. A planet crossing it on Earth's side is at opposition; on the far side, at conjunction. The ecliptic ring shows the same sky from Earth. The arc from the Sun to the Moon is the lunar phase as an angle, and the shaded arcs around the Moon's nodes are the eclipse seasons.

      IV · Recursus

      Recurrence

      This is a stroboscope. It samples one cycle each time another returns to phase 0, plotting time outward from the centre. If q periods of one nearly equal p of the other, the samples fall into q spiral arms. The continued-fraction table lists these near-recurrences, each more exact and longer than the last. That is a nested hierarchy of approximate commensurabilities in a quasi-periodic system, which is not the same thing as fractal structure.

      Phases

      Phase portrait

      Two phases make a point on a torus: cycle A runs around the ring and cycle B around the tube. As time passes the point winds around the surface. If the ratio of periods were a fraction, the path would close; because it is not, the path never quite closes and slowly covers the whole surface. The amber circle is where A returns to phase 0. The points on it are the stroboscope samples above, a Poincaré section of the motion. Drag the torus to turn it.
